00 · How this page works

Try it, then the reasons, then the limits.

Every concept has the same five parts, in the same order: the thing to try, why it works, where it comes from, its limits, and questions to ponder. That order is John Hutchinson's idea, from his Rice University chemistry course: look at the evidence first, and build the explanation last.

Under each concept, every source has an author, a year, how they found it out, and a link. Tier 1 means peer-reviewed research, or the author's own text, that we opened. When the evidence is weaker, we say so next to it. The rules are on how we check.

Every example below is invented and labelled as one. Nothing you type leaves your browser.

Earlier versions of this site used other names for five of these ideas. Why the names changed →

C1 · Pull it back out

What you can bring back is what you hold.

Retrieval, spacing and sleep. The strongest-evidenced idea on this page, and the one most study habits skip.

Try this first

Take the 60-second test on the home page: read one paragraph, rate how sure you feel, then answer with it hidden. Or try the twelve-letter chunking test on Career.

On paper: read a paragraph of anything you're learning, close it, and write three things it said. Check. The blanks are the lesson.

On a weekday

Close the book or the tab and write what you remember before you reread anything. Do it again a day or two later. Sleep in between.

Why it works

Rereading makes text familiar, and familiar feels like known. Pulling it back out with the page closed is harder, and that effort is what makes it last. Spreading the practice out over days beats one long sitting.

Where it comes from

  1. Roediger and Karpicke (2006), Psychological Science. Lab experiments with students and prose passages. Tested students remembered more after two days and a week than restudy students, though restudy did better at five minutes. doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.xTier 1
  2. Dunlosky and colleagues (2013), Psychological Science in the Public Interest. A review of ten learning techniques. Practice testing and spreading practice out were the two rated most useful; highlighting and rereading were rated low. doi:10.1177/1529100612453266Tier 1 · review
  3. Cepeda and colleagues (2006), Psychological Bulletin. A quantitative review of spaced practice in verbal recall tasks. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354Tier 1 · meta-analysis
  4. Rasch and Born (2013), Physiological Reviews. A review of sleep's role in memory. doi:10.1152/physrev.00032.2012Tier 1 · review

Further learning, not evidence: Barbara Oakley and Terrence Sejnowski's free course Learning How to Learn.

Limits

  • Most of this comes from lab and classroom tasks.
  • The sleep evidence is mostly about mechanisms, not about a study routine.

Questions to ponder

  • What did you "learn" last week that you couldn't write down now?

C2 · Anchor memory

What will still be there tomorrow?

Two parts. The first is for learning, and it has the strongest evidence on this page. The second is optional, for a rushed moment, and its evidence is weaker.

Try this first · a learning anchor

Welcome back

You left a note here on . Don't look yet. Write it again from memory, as close as you can get.

Then

Now, from memory

Look at what survived and what didn't. What came back is what you actually hold. What didn't come back wasn't stored as well as it felt when you wrote it. Bringing it back just now was practice too: pulling something out of memory helps it last longer than reading it again.

Take a scrap of paper. Write one idea from today in two or three lines, with one example and one link to your own life. Put the paper away. Tomorrow, write it again before you look.

What we could spot

The three parts

    Vague words

    This is a crude word check, not a reader. It looks for an example word or a number, and for "I", "my" or "we". You're the real judge.

    The point

    Right now it probably feels solid. That feeling isn't a good guide. In the study this idea rests on, rereading did better than testing after five minutes. Testing only pulled ahead after two days. So the real check is tomorrow, after a night's sleep.

    Optional · a steadying anchor Weaker evidence

    Some people keep one specific good memory ready for a rushed moment. Make it concrete: a place, a person, and one sentence that was said. Try it now, in your head. Nothing here is saved.

    When a message or a person is rushing you, bring that memory up before you act. Think of it as a pause, not a cure. Two studies point this way, one in a lab and one that tracked teenagers over a year. Both are listed below. Neither shows that it treats anything.

    If you're struggling right now, a memory exercise isn't enough on its own. Get help now lists free lines, each checked on the organisation's own page.

    Detail is a clue, not proof

    A memory with a place, a sound and someone's words in it is more likely to be something you lived than something you only read about. But every time you recall a memory, you rebuild it, so detail can be added later. Use detail as a reason to look closer. Don't treat it as a lie detector.

    On a weekday

    Close the tab on one thing you learned. The next morning, write it again without looking: the idea, one example, one link to your life. Then check it against the source. The gap is the lesson.

    Why it works

    What you can rebuild later, without looking, is what you actually hold. The feeling of knowing is a poor guide, because rereading makes text familiar, and familiar feels like known. Pulling an idea back out after a gap is what helps it last, and sleep in between helps it settle. An example and a link to yourself give the idea more to hang on.

    Where it comes from

    1. Roediger and Karpicke (2006), Psychological Science. Lab experiments with students and short prose passages. Students who were tested on a passage remembered more after two days and after a week than students who studied it again. At five minutes, the restudy group did better. Restudying made students more confident. doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.xTier 1 · the core study for this idea
    2. Rasch and Born (2013), Physiological Reviews. A long review of research on sleep and how memories are consolidated. doi:10.1152/physrev.00032.2012Tier 1 · review
    3. Symons and Johnson (1997), Psychological Bulletin. A meta-analysis of studies on linking material to yourself, building on Rogers, Kuiper and Kirker (1977, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.35.9.677). Linking things to yourself tends to help memory. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.121.3.371Tier 1 · meta-analysis
    4. Weinstein, Madan and Sumeracki (2018), Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications. A review of learning strategies for teachers. Concrete examples are among the well-supported ones. doi:10.1186/s41235-017-0087-yTier 1 · review
    5. Johnson and Raye (1981), Psychological Review. A theory paper on how we tell real events from imagined ones: memories of real events tend to carry more sensory and contextual detail. doi:10.1037/0033-295X.88.1.67Tier 1 · theory
    6. Schacter and Addis (2007), Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B. A review arguing that memory is constructive rather than reproductive: recall rebuilds, and it can go wrong. See also Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay (1993, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.114.1.3) on mistaking fiction for fact. doi:10.1098/rstb.2007.2087Tier 1 · review
    7. For the steadying anchor: Speer and Delgado (2017), Nature Human Behaviour. Two lab studies. After a stress task, recalling positive memories, but not neutral ones, led to a smaller rise in the stress hormone cortisol and less negative mood. doi:10.1038/s41562-017-0093Tier 1 · lab stress task, small samples
    8. For the steadying anchor: Askelund and colleagues (2019), Nature Human Behaviour. 427 teenagers at risk of depression. Those who recalled more specific positive memories had lower morning cortisol and fewer negative thoughts about themselves a year later. This is a correlation, not a test of the exercise. doi:10.1038/s41562-018-0504-3Tier 1 · correlational

    Further learning, not evidence: Barbara Oakley and Terrence Sejnowski's free course Learning How to Learn covers retrieval and sleep for a general audience. The research behind those points is the papers above.

    Limits

    • The advantage only showed up after a delay. Judge this idea tomorrow, not tonight.
    • Detail doesn't prove a memory is real. Recall rebuilds. One preprint (Thakral and colleagues, 2018, doi:10.31234/osf.io/j8w96, not peer reviewed, Tier 3) suggests that practising detail can even add false detail.
    • We found no study showing that a vivid personal memory protects you from AI-made content. An earlier version of this page implied it did. We've removed that.
    • The steadying anchor rests on lab stress tasks with small samples, and one correlational study of teenagers. It isn't a treatment.

    Questions to ponder

    • If you can rebuild a memory in rich detail, how would you know whether some of that detail was added later?
    • Is a summary ever enough? When does a summary serve you better than a scene?

      C3 · Name the feeling

      What did your thumb decide before you did?

      A pressure message is built to make a feeling decide for you. This one is a simulation.

      Do this first

      Simulation · invented message No money moves, nothing is sent, nothing is recorded. Imagine it landed on your phone.

      ?
      +91 9•••• ••417
      Not in your contacts · voice note + text
      ▶ 0:14
      "Beta, it's me, Mama. My phone fell in water, I'm on a friend's number. Don't tell Papa yet, he'll only worry. I'm at the hospital billing counter and they won't start until it's paid."
      voice note · transcript
      Send ₹20,000 to billing.help@exampleupi before 6. Please beta, quickly 🙏
      5:51 PM

      0:20 · reply needed

      What do you do?

      You chose

      Before you read on, name the feeling in one word. What was in your chest?

      How the message was built

      • A familiar voice: voice-cloning tools can imitate a voice from short recordings. The security vendor McAfee says in its own 2023 release that in one instance three seconds of audio was enough for an 85% voice match, by its researchers' own benchmark (McAfee, 1 May 2023). That is a vendor's claim, not an independent test, and it's here only as context. Either way, a familiar sound alone is no longer proof of who's speaking.
      • A new number: the story explains away the one thing you'd normally check.
      • "Don't tell Papa": it cuts off the person most likely to check.
      • Hospital: fear and duty together. You're supposed to feel that holding back is cruel.
      • "Before 6": a deadline, so the feeling decides before you do.

      None of these is new. Urgency, secrecy and a loved one in trouble are older than writing. What's new is the voice, and the price: a stranger can make a thousand of these before lunch.

      The move: name the feeling, then call the person on the number you already had. Naming it buys you a pause. The call back is what protects you: it settles the question either way. If it's real, you've reached Mama. If it isn't, you've lost nothing.

      On a weekday

      The pause before you pay, forward or reply. Name the feeling in one word, in whatever language you feel it in: scared, rushed, guilty, flattered. Then check through a channel you already trust, not the one in the message. If you can't name it, don't send it yet.

      Why it works

      Pressure messages work by letting a strong feeling crowd out checking. Putting the feeling into a plain word is a small, cheap way to turn its volume down. In studies, the clearest effects show up in the brain and the body. Whether people say they feel calmer is less consistent. So naming buys you a moment. What protects you is the second step: the call back on a number you already had. That call settles the question either way.

      Where it comes from

      1. Lieberman and colleagues (2007), Psychological Science. A brain-imaging study. Putting feelings into words lowered the response of the amygdala, a part of the brain involved in threat. doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.xTier 1
      2. Torre and Lieberman (2018), Emotion Review. A review of affect labelling across feelings, the body, the brain and behaviour. doi:10.1177/1754073917742706Tier 1 · review
      3. Lieberman, Inagaki, Tabibnia and Crockett (2011), Emotion. Experiments: people didn't expect labelling to help, yet it reduced the distress they reported about as much as reappraisal and distraction did. doi:10.1037/a0023503Tier 1
      4. Kircanski, Lieberman and Craske (2012), Psychological Science. An exposure-therapy experiment. A week later, the group that labelled their feelings had lower skin conductance, a body measure of arousal. Self-reported fear didn't differ. doi:10.1177/0956797612443830Tier 1
      5. Kashdan, Barrett and McKnight (2015), Current Directions in Psychological Science. A review on emotional granularity: finer words for feelings go with better ways of coping. doi:10.1177/0963721414550708Tier 1 · review
      6. Martel, Pennycook and Rand (2020), Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications. One correlational study (N = 409) and four experiments (N = 3,884). Inducing people to rely on emotion raised belief in fake news, but not in real news. doi:10.1186/s41235-020-00252-3Tier 1

      Context, not evidence for this idea: the McAfee figure in the message breakdown above comes from a vendor's own 2023 survey and benchmark. It's there to show how voice-clone scams work, not to show that naming a feeling helps.

      Limits

      • Much of the evidence comes from one lab.
      • An independent team didn't replicate the effect on reported negative feeling: Vlasenko, Rogers and Waugh (2021), Cognition and Emotion, four studies, 405 people (doi:10.1080/02699931.2021.1959302).
      • Language may matter. In a small study of 26 bilingual people, labelling in a foreign language didn't lower amygdala activity: Vives and colleagues (2021), Affective Science (doi:10.1007/s42761-021-00039-9).
      • We found no study showing that naming a feeling during a scam makes people less likely to pay. The call back is what does that work.

      Questions to ponder

      • Which feeling do you most often fail to notice? Do urgency and duty feel different in your body?
      • Would you label it in English, or in the language your mother uses?

      C4 · Notice the pattern

      What isn't the piece saying?

      Two patterns to look for: what you've heard many times, and what's missing.

      Do this first
      Quick, before you read: should schools ban phones?

      Invented example The school, the people and the numbers are all made up.

      Phone ban sends results soaring at city school

      A year after Greenfield Public School locked away students' phones during the school day, its Class 10 pass rate has jumped from 61% to 78%. "Children are finally paying attention," said the principal, who introduced the ban. Parents we spoke to were delighted. Other schools are now considering the same rule.

      How much of that jump do you think the ban caused?
      What would you want to know that the piece doesn't tell you? Pick any.

      What the piece left out still invented

      • Fewer students sat the exam. The school stopped entering students it expected to fail, so 180 students sat the year before and 120 sat this year.
      • Everyone went up. The board changed the paper pattern that year, and pass rates rose across the district, at schools with bans and without.
      • One voice. The only person quoted is the principal who brought in the ban. No teachers, no students, no one from the board.

      Now, same question: how much of the jump did the ban cause?

      What moved

      Every sentence in the piece could be true. What changed your reading was what wasn't there. Noticing the pattern means asking two things: what have I heard many times, and what would the missing side say? The hardest place to ask is a story that agrees with you.

      On a weekday

      When a claim feels obviously true, ask whether you checked it or just heard it a lot. Take one piece you agree with and write the sentence the other side would add. Take one piece you reject and write the fact that would change your mind. Do this on your own reading, not just on other people's.

      Why it works

      Your sense of what's true is partly a sense of what's familiar, and repetition makes things familiar. You also judge the evidence in front of you and rarely weigh what you weren't shown. Asking for the missing side is a tested fix. In one study it greatly reduced the bias from seeing only one side of a case.

      Where it comes from

      1. Hasher, Goldstein and Toppino (1977), Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior. 60 plausible statements, rated in three sessions two weeks apart. Statements that came back were rated more likely to be true, whether they were true or not. doi:10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80012-1Tier 1
      2. Dechêne and colleagues (2010), Personality and Social Psychology Review. A meta-analysis of this "repetition makes it feel true" effect. doi:10.1177/1088868309352251Tier 1 · meta-analysis
      3. Fazio and colleagues (2015), Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. Experiments: repetition raised belief even in statements people had the knowledge to reject. doi:10.1037/xge0000098Tier 1
      4. Pennycook, Cannon and Rand (2018), Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. Experiments with fake news headlines. Seeing a headline once made it feel more accurate a week later, even with a "disputed" warning on it. It didn't hold for entirely implausible statements. doi:10.1037/xge0000465Tier 1
      5. Brenner, Koehler and Tversky (1996), Journal of Behavioral Decision Making. Experiments with legal cases. People who saw only one side were more confident but generally less accurate. Asking them to rate the other side's case greatly reduced the bias. That's the weekday drill above. doi:10.1002/(SICI)1099-0771(199603)9:1<59::AID-BDM216>3.0.CO;2-VTier 1
      6. Tversky and Kahneman (1981), Science. Choice experiments: the same facts, framed differently, changed what people chose. doi:10.1126/science.7455683Tier 1
      7. A real case of what's left out: Turner and colleagues (2008), New England Journal of Medicine. Of 74 antidepressant trials registered with the US FDA, 94% looked positive in the published literature. In the FDA's own analysis, 51% were positive. doi:10.1056/NEJMsa065779Tier 1

      Limits

      • Most of this comes from lab studies with trivia statements or headlines, not from real news diets.
      • Repetition has a smaller effect on claims that are entirely implausible.
      • The school article above is invented. It shows the pattern. It isn't evidence.

      Questions to ponder

      • Which belief do you hold mainly because you've heard it often?
      • In the last article you agreed with, whose voice was missing?

      C5 · Trace the source

      Follow the number back to where it started.

      A viral claim, one hop at a time, back to the first document.

      Do this first

      Invented example Riverton, its council and its newspaper don't exist. The chain is built to show a pattern.

      What reached you · forwarded many times · no source

      🚨 EXPERTS WARN: HALF of Riverton's trees will be CUT DOWN by 2030. Share before this gets deleted!!

      Before you trace it: roughly how many trees are actually going to be cut down?

      Hop 4 · a blog post · cites the Riverton Daily

      "Riverton to lose half its trees, report warns. Experts say the city's green cover will be cut in half by 2030."

      "Trees on the ring road" became "the city's trees". "Affected" became "lose". "Experts" appeared out of nowhere.

      Hop 3 · Riverton Daily · cites a council press note

      "Ring-road plan could affect up to half of trees, council says. Works are due to finish by 2030."

      "On the 4 km stretch" was dropped. "Most will be transplanted" was dropped.

      Hop 2 · council press note · cites the tree survey

      "The ring-road widening affects 50% of trees on the 4 km stretch. Most affected trees are marked for transplanting. Works complete by 2030."

      "206 trees inside the widening line" became "50% affected". The split between transplanting and removal shrank to the word "most".

      Hop 1 · the first source · council tree survey, field sheet

      Trees surveyed, ring road, 4 km stretch412
      Inside the widening line206
      Marked for transplanting160
      Marked for removal46

      At the first source

      46 trees marked for removal, on one 4 km stretch.

      The forward said half the city. Nobody in the chain told an outright lie. Each hop rounded something off, dropped a qualifier or added a word, and every edit leaned toward the more alarming version.

      That's how a claim mutates. It doesn't usually get forged. It gets compressed, one plausible step at a time. The only defence is to keep going back until you reach a table, a recording or a named person with a date.

      On a weekday

      Go back. A quote has a speaker and a date. A number has a table. A photo has a place, or it doesn't. Stop at the first source, not at the article that cites the article. Before you read a page closely, open a new tab and find out who is behind it. If you can't find the first source at all, that's your answer.

      Why it works

      Each retelling can drop a qualifier and add certainty. Judging the copy by how it looks tells you little, because looking credible is cheap. Going upstream to the original table, document or recording, and checking who is speaking, is quicker than reading closely and more reliable.

      Where it comes from

      1. Wineburg and McGrew (2019), Teachers College Record. A think-aloud study of 45 people: 10 historians, 10 professional fact-checkers and 25 university students, judging real websites. The fact-checkers left the page early to see what others said about the source ("lateral reading"). They reached better-founded conclusions in a fraction of the time. doi:10.1177/016146811912101102Tier 1
      2. Mike Caulfield (2019), "SIFT (The Four Moves)", on his own blog. Stop; investigate the source; find better coverage; trace claims to the original context. A teaching method, not a study. hapgood.us, 19 Jun 2019Tier 1 · the author's own text
      3. Wineburg and colleagues (2022), Journal of Educational Psychology. A field study of six lateral-reading lessons: 271 students who had them, compared with 228 who didn't. doi:10.1037/edu0000740Tier 1
      4. Breakstone and colleagues (2021), Harvard Kennedy School Misinformation Review (doi:10.37016/mr-2020-56), and Brodsky and colleagues (2021), Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications (doi:10.1186/s41235-021-00291-4). Both tested lateral-reading teaching with college students.Tier 1
      5. A real case of a claim growing on the way: Sumner and colleagues (2014), BMJ. 462 university press releases about health research and 668 news stories. 33% of the releases overstated a causal claim. When the release overstated, 81% of the news stories did too, against 18% when it didn't. doi:10.1136/bmj.g7015Tier 1 · observational

      For further reading: Caulfield and Wineburg's book Verified (University of Chicago Press, 2023, doi:10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.001.0001). We've confirmed it exists, but haven't reviewed it, so nothing on this page rests on it.

      Limits

      • The founding study is a small think-aloud study, 45 people in all.
      • How long the skill lasts after it's taught is still unclear.
      • The press-release study is observational, and covers health news only.
      • Riverton's trees are invented. The chain shows a pattern. It isn't evidence.

      Questions to ponder

      • Where did the last statistic you repeated come from? Did you see the original?
      • When is "a reputable outlet says so" a good enough place to stop?

      C6 · Look first

      Build the model last, and watch it break.

      Observations first, the model last. This order comes from John Hutchinson's chemistry course at Rice University.

      Do this first
      1. Foundation
      2. Question
      3. Observations
      4. Deduction
      5. Model

      Foundation. Objects put in water either float or sink, and doing the same thing twice gives the same result.
      Question. What decides whether something floats?

      Observations (all things you can try in a kitchen)

      • An iron nail sinks.
      • A coin sinks.
      • A small stone sinks.
      • A wooden spoon floats.
      • A cork floats.
      Your model. Which rule explains all five?

      A new observation

      The last observation, for every model

      An egg sinks in a glass of tap water. Stir in plenty of salt and the same egg floats.

      Same egg, so the model now has to include the liquid too. The rule that survives is: compare the object, including anything it encloses, with the same volume of the liquid it's in. That one has lasted a long time. It still isn't proved. It just hasn't been broken yet.

      What just happened

      Each model you held was reasonable for the observations you had, and each one broke on the next observation. That isn't a failure. It's what models are for. If you'd been handed "density" on the first line, you could have recited it without knowing which observation it answers, or what would make you give it up.

      This is our kitchen example set out in Hutchinson's order. It isn't one of his studies. His studies apply the same order to chemistry.

      On a weekday

      Write the observation in a sentence that doesn't contain the conclusion. Then the question. Then the model, and what would break it. If all you can do is state the model, you accepted it. You didn't build it. The Career module has the full order →

      Why it works

      A model you're handed first, you can recite without knowing what it explains. A model you build from observations comes with its reasons, and with a sense of what would break it. Trying a problem before you're taught tends to make the teaching land better, as long as the teaching does follow.

      Where it comes from

      1. John S. Hutchinson, Concept Development Studies in Chemistry, Rice University, free and open (also listed in the Open Textbook Library). Each study starts from a foundation, then questions, then observations and deductions, and only then a model. In his words, the collection is meant to "redirect the focus of learning." This is a course design, not an experiment. We found no published test of his course, so the order is his design, not something he measured.Tier 1 · the author's own text
      2. Sinha and Kapur (2021), Review of Educational Research. A meta-analysis of studies where students tried problems before being taught ("productive failure"), compared with being taught first. Overall effect in favour of trying first: g = 0.36. doi:10.3102/00346543211019105Tier 1 · meta-analysis
      3. Kapur (2008), Cognition and Instruction (doi:10.1080/07370000802212669), and Kapur (2016), Educational Psychologist (doi:10.1080/00461520.2016.1155457). The original productive-failure study, and a later paper that separates productive from unproductive failure.Tier 1
      4. Schwartz and Bransford (1998), Cognition and Instruction (doi:10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4), and Schwartz and Martin (2004), Cognition and Instruction (doi:10.1207/s1532690xci2202_1). Studies of students trying a problem themselves before being told the answer.Tier 1
      5. Chinn and Brewer (1993), Review of Educational Research. A review of how people respond to data that doesn't fit their theory. It sets out seven responses. Only one of them is to accept the data and change the theory. doi:10.3102/00346543063001001Tier 1 · review

      The other side of the debate: Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006), "Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work", Educational Psychologist (doi:10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1). We've confirmed the paper and its title, but haven't reviewed its argument, so we name it as the counterweight without citing it for any finding.

      Limits

      • Trying first works best when clear teaching follows it. This isn't a case for working everything out on your own.
      • People are good at explaining away an observation that doesn't fit. The egg only breaks your model if you let it.
      • No one has published a test of Hutchinson's own course that we could find. The evidence above is for the general approach.
      • Our floating example is a kitchen demonstration. It isn't one of Hutchinson's studies.

      Questions to ponder

      • Which rule do you "know" but couldn't rebuild from the observations?
      • What would you have to see to drop a model you rely on?

      C7 · Pause before you share or act

      A fast answer is a first draft.

      Judging takes time and attention. Pressure pushes you onto the first impression. A second look fixes many of the mistakes the first one makes.

      Try this first

      Run the "Two answers" lab on Money: six invented messages, answered once against a 5-second clock and once with no clock. Count how many answers you change.

      On paper: next time a message asks you to pay, forward or reply now, write your first answer, wait one minute, and write your second. Compare.

      On a weekday

      Before you forward, pay or reply, ask one question: is this accurate? If a clock is running, the clock is the first thing to doubt. The official slogan from the Prime Minister's Mann Ki Baat of 27 Oct 2024 says it in three steps: "Ruko, Socho, Action Lo" (stop, think, then act).

      Why it works

      Much of the time people share or act on something false not because they believe it, but because their attention was somewhere else. A small prompt that turns attention back to accuracy improves what they share. Giving the same people time to think again corrects many intuitive mistakes about false headlines.

      Where it comes from

      1. Bago, Rand and Pennycook (2020), Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. 1,635 online participants judged headlines twice: first under time pressure with a memory load, then with no limits. They believed false headlines (not true ones) more in the fast first answer. doi:10.1037/xge0000729Tier 1
      2. Pennycook and colleagues (2021), Nature. Four survey experiments (N = 3,485) and a Twitter field experiment (N = 5,379). Shifting attention to accuracy raised the quality of the news people then shared. doi:10.1038/s41586-021-03344-2Tier 1
      3. Pennycook and Rand (2022), Nature Communications. An internal meta-analysis of 20 experiments (N = 26,863). Accuracy prompts cut sharing intentions for false headlines by about 10% relative to control. doi:10.1038/s41467-022-30073-5Tier 1 · meta-analysis by the same group
      4. Arechar and colleagues (2023), Nature Human Behaviour. Misinformation and accuracy prompts across 16 countries on six continents. doi:10.1038/s41562-023-01641-6Tier 1

      ✋ Feel the number

      "About 10% lower" is a share of intentions, not of people. Out of every 100 false headlines a group would have said they'd share, a prompt left about 90. Small for one person, large across millions of forwards. Our arithmetic on Pennycook and Rand (2022).

      Limits

      • A preregistered replication failed at its first stage (n = 701) and found a small effect only after pooling more data (Roozenbeek and colleagues, 2021, Psychological Science, doi:10.1177/09567976211024535).
      • Nudges shrink at scale: in 126 trials by two government nudge units, the average effect was much smaller than in academic papers (DellaVigna and Linos, 2022, Econometrica, doi:10.3982/ECTA18709).
      • All of these are studies of headlines and sharing. Carrying them over to scam calls and payments is our inference.

      Questions to ponder

      • If a call is genuine, what do you lose by hanging up and calling back on a number you found yourself?

      C8 · Ask who benefits

      Who gets paid if you say yes?

      Every pitch has an author with a goal. Naming the goal turns a message into a move you can see.

      Try this first

      Invented example A "free" webinar on trading ends with a paid VIP group. Before you think about the tips, write down every payment that is certain to happen if you join, and who receives it.

      What most people find

      The only certain payment is your joining fee, to the host. The returns are a promise. The fee is a fact.

      On a weekday

      For any offer, pitch or warning: who wrote this, what do they want me to do, and what do they get if I do it? Then judge the claim.

      Why it works

      People build up knowledge about persuaders' goals and tactics and use it to cope. Asking "who benefits?" puts that knowledge to work at the moment it matters.

      Where it comes from

      1. Friestad and Wright (1994), Journal of Consumer Research. The Persuasion Knowledge Model: people develop and use knowledge of persuasion agents' goals and tactics to cope with persuasion attempts. doi:10.1086/209380Tier 1 · theory
      2. Cialdini and Goldstein (2004), Annual Review of Psychology. A review of research on compliance and conformity. doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.142015Tier 1 · review
      3. Stajano and Wilson (2011), Communications of the ACM, "Understanding scam victims". From studying real scams, seven principles scammers exploit: Distraction, Social Compliance, Herd, Dishonesty, Kindness, Need and Greed, and Time. doi:10.1145/1897852.1897872Tier 1 · field observation

      Limits

      • These are a theory, a review and field observation. We found no trial showing that asking "who benefits?" protects people. We label it "theory + review".

      • Who benefits from the last warning you forwarded?

      C9 · Compare it to something

      Big compared with what?

      A number on its own says little. Ask for the base rate, the denominator, and the number it should sit next to.

      Try this first

      Invented numbers An app flags 9 in 10 scam calls and wrongly flags 1 in 100 genuine calls. 1 in 100 of your calls is a scam. When it flags a call, how likely is it to be a scam?

      Count it out

      Take 10,000 calls. 100 are scams, and 90 of those are flagged. 9,900 are genuine, and 99 of those are flagged. So 90 of 189 flags are scams: about half. Most people guess about 9 in 10, because they forget how rare scams are.

      On a weekday

      Turn percentages into counts ("out of 1,000 people…"). Ask what the number is a share of. Never divide one figure by another from a different period or measure.

      Why it works

      People tend to drop base rates when other information feels more specific. Counts in natural frequencies make the base rate visible, and many more people reason correctly with them.

      Where it comes from

      1. Bar-Hillel (1980), Acta Psychologica, "The base-rate fallacy in probability judgments". People drop base rates when other information seems more specific or causal. doi:10.1016/0001-6918(80)90046-3Tier 1
      2. Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995), Psychological Review. With frequency formats, people with no training solved up to 50% of Bayesian problems correctly. doi:10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684Tier 1
      3. Turner and colleagues (2008), New England Journal of Medicine. Compare published results with all results: 94% of FDA-registered antidepressant trials looked positive in the literature; 51% were positive in the FDA's analysis. doi:10.1056/NEJMsa065779Tier 1

      Limits

      • The effects are shown in lab problems. How well people carry the skill into daily life isn't shown.

      • The last big number you read in the news: what was it a share of?
