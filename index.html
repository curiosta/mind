How to learn, and how not to be fooled.

Information is cheap now. A finished answer is cheaper still. What has become scarce is a memory you actually lived, and a claim you can trace back to a person, a date, and a method.

We won't ask you to believe that. Give us sixty seconds and you'll see it for yourself.

01 · Try this first

Read one paragraph. Then we take it away.

No timer, no trick questions. Read it the way you'd read anything you wanted to learn.

60-second test

Step 1 · Read

Your brain has two ways of working. In focused mode you work on a problem you already have a frame for. In diffuse mode the mind relaxes and links ideas you did not mean to link. You cannot be in both at once. So work for twenty-five minutes, then take a walk, a bus ride, or a shower. The idea that turns up in the shower is not magic: focused work has to leave an unfinished problem behind, or diffuse mode has nothing to chew on. And the feeling of knowing is not proof. Rereading and highlighting feel like study, but what they give you is recognition. The test is whether you can bring it back with the page closed.

This paragraph is our short reading of Barbara Oakley and Terrence Sejnowski's course Learning How to Learn.

Before we hide it: how sure are you that you could explain this to a friend right now?

Step 2 · Bring it back, page closed

Type a few words, or just answer in your head and press the button. Nobody sees this but you.

Step 3 · Mark yourself honestly

What just happened

Remember how you felt while the paragraph was on the screen? That was recognition. What you just did with the page closed was recall. Oakley calls the gap between the two an illusion of competence. Rereading, highlighting and a fluent explanation from a chatbot all leave you feeling the first one.

The fix is the thing you just did: close it, rebuild it, look at the gap. Sleep on it and try again tomorrow. Spacing it out works better than one long sitting.

See the modules

Now cover the paragraph with your hand and answer these out loud:

  1. What are the two modes called?
    AnswerFocused and diffuse.
  2. For the idea to turn up in the shower, what does focused work have to leave behind?
    AnswerAn unfinished problem.
  3. Rereading and highlighting feel like study. What do they actually give you?
    AnswerRecognition, not recall.

If one of those was harder than it felt while you were reading, you've just met the illusion of competence. Reading it gave you recognition. Answering with it covered took recall. Close it, rebuild it, look at the gap.

02 · Why that matters now

Two failures, same week.

You ask a model to explain a chapter. It does, fluently, and you nod along because the explanation sounds right. The next morning you cannot rebuild the argument without the screen in front of you. That is the gap you just felt. It is borrowing, not learning, and the bill comes due the moment the tool is closed.

Someone forwards a voice note. It sounds like a relative. The account is old, the request is urgent, and you almost send the money before you catch yourself. That is not a new trick. Urgency and a familiar voice are older than writing. The machine only made them cheap to produce at scale.

mind is for both failures. How to learn when answers are free. How not to be fooled by a machine, or by a person.

04 · Today

One pass. Ten minutes.

  1. Write one memory from yesterday that a stranger could picture. Smells, words, hands. Not "it was nice."
  2. Pick one claim you almost forwarded. Who said it first? If you cannot name them, do not send it.
  3. Close the tab on something you "already know." Write three lines from memory. The gap is the lesson.

The science under this comes from Barbara Oakley and Terrence Sejnowski (Learning How to Learn, A Mind for Numbers) and John Hutchinson (Concept Development Studies in Chemistry, Rice University). The plain names and the mapping are ours. The method is theirs. Career module →
