Rereading: why does it feel like studying?

The home page has you try a 60-second version of this on yourself. This case file asks a different question: what does the published evidence say about the habit?

The claim

Reading my notes again is a good way to study.

Tier 1 · Primary Last checked 24 Oct 2026 (IST)

Why this tier: Peer-reviewed papers, each opened by DOI. We read the abstracts (14 Oct 2026) and re-checked the DOIs today. We did not re-read the full papers. What the tiers mean is on how we check.

The clues

What the clues say.

Clues against the claim

  • Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) reviewed study techniques and rated practice testing and distributed practice as “high utility”, and rereading and highlighting as “low”.
  • Roediger and Karpicke (2006): after a week, people who had been tested recalled more than people who had only re-studied the text. We quote no percentages here. The abstract has none and we haven't re-read the tables.
  • Rowland's (2014) meta-analysis of retrieval practice found testing beat restudy overall. We quote no effect size, for the same reason.

Clues about why it feels like it works

  • Koriat and Bjork (2005) describe “illusions of competence”: material that is easy to read can feel learned when it is not.
  • Rereading is easy and familiar, so it can feel like progress. How you feel while studying and what you can do later can come apart.

The doubts

What we can't tell.

  • “Low utility” is a rating from a review of the evidence. It is not a ruling that rereading never helps.
  • These are mostly lab and classroom memory tests. They don't tell us how it plays out in your subject or your exam.
  • We have not looked at studies of Indian students for this case.
  • The home-page test is one person and one paragraph. It is a demonstration, not an experiment.

Where the clues point

Rereading alone looks like a weak plan, and closing the book and recalling looks stronger. “Weak” is not “useless”, and these papers can't tell you how much it matters for you. This is a leaning, not a verdict. It changes if the clues do.

Try it first: the 60-second test, then the Career module.

Questions to ponder

Over to you.

  1. Think of your last study session. How much was reading again, and how much was closing the book and writing what you remembered?
  2. If rereading feels easy and recalling feels hard, which feeling is evidence of learning? Why might it be the harder one?
  3. What would count as evidence to you that a method works: how you feel afterwards, or what you can do a week later?
