“Digital arrest”: who is on the other end of the call?

This is a case about a phone call. Nothing here is about any one person. It is about what the official record says and what it can't tell us.

The claim

A police or CBI officer can put you under “digital arrest” on a video call until you comply.

Tier 1 · Primary Last checked 26 Oct 2026 (IST)

Why this tier: The statements come from the Government of India's own press release and the Prime Minister's address, which we opened. The loss figures are from a written reply to Parliament (Ministry of Home Affairs). The Ministry's PDF of that reply did not open on 26 Oct 2026, so the loss figures rest on a media report (Tier 2). The PIB release of the same reply, which we did open, gives the other numbers but not the loss table. These are official statements, and we found no independent audit of them. What the tiers mean is on how we check.

The clues

What the clues say.

Clues that the claim is false

  • PIB, 27 Oct 2024: the Home Minister's post says the fraudsters' method “is to pose as police, CBI, anti-narcotics or RBI officers and threaten unsuspecting citizens over a video call”, and the release says no government agency conducts investigations via phone or video calls.
  • The Prime Minister's address (Mann Ki Baat, 27 Oct 2024, PMO text; the PMO's page titles it the 115th episode) says: “You should be aware that no investigative agency never inquires like this through a phone call or a video call.” That is the PMO's published translation, awkward double negative included.
  • The Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre advisory TAU-ADV-003 (6 Mar 2025) is reported to say “digital arrest” has no legal basis. Tier 2 We could not open the PDF (our checker was served the site's home page instead), so we can only say the advisory is listed in search results, not what it says word for word.

Clues about how the call is built and how big it is

  • The PMO text names time pressure as the lever: “You have to decide now or else you will be arrested.”
  • MHA's reply in the Rajya Sabha (12 Mar 2025), as reported by ETGovernment, gives losses reported on the national cybercrime portal for digital-arrest scams and related cybercrimes: ₹91.14 crore (39,925 incidents) in 2022, ₹1,935.51 crore (1,23,672 incidents) in 2024, and ₹210.21 crore (17,718 incidents) from 1 Jan to 28 Feb 2025. Tier 2 These figures are reported by media, official PDF not reachable on 26 Oct 2026. The 2024 figure is about 21 times the 2022 one (our arithmetic: 1,935.51 ÷ 91.14; ETGovernment also says 21 times).
  • The PIB release of the same reply (12 Mar 2025, opened by us, Tier 1) says I4C identified and blocked more than 3,962 Skype IDs and 83,668 WhatsApp accounts used for digital arrest, and that the Prime Minister spoke about digital arrests in Mann Ki Baat on 27 Oct 2024.

The doubts

What we can't tell.

  • These are statements by the Government of India about what its own agencies do. We haven't found an outside check of them.
  • The figures count complaints on a national portal. Rising numbers could mean more scams, more reporting, or both. Nothing we read separates the two.
  • The Ministry's PDF of the Rajya Sabha reply returned 404 on 26 Oct 2026, from two addresses. We have not seen the loss table in the original. The PIB release of the same reply leaves the table out, so the loss figures rest on one media report (ETGovernment). Its numbers match the reply's text as a search tool showed it to us, which is not the same as opening the file.
  • We don't know how “digital arrest” was defined when cases were counted.

Where the clues point

The claim is false as stated: the official line is that agencies don't arrest or investigate by phone or video call. The loss trend is large, but it measures reported complaints, not how many people were fooled. This is a leaning, not a verdict. It changes if the clues do.

See it in practice: the voice-note drill on the spells page and Would you act?. If you have already lost money, Get help now lists the national cybercrime line as the government states it.

Questions to ponder

Over to you.

  1. If a call like this reached you, what number that you already had could you use to check it, without using anything the caller gave you?
  2. Rising complaint numbers: what would you need to see to tell more scams from more reporting?
  3. Why would a deadline appear in so many descriptions of these calls? What does “decide now” take away?
