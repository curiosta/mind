Prebunking: do short videos protect people from manipulation?

“Protect” is a big word. This case file asks how much of it the evidence carries.

The claim

A short video can protect people from online manipulation.

Tier 1 · Primary Last checked 24 Oct 2026 (IST)

Why this tier: The main study is a peer-reviewed paper opened by DOI. The India clue is a preprint (Tier 3) that we have seen only in summary, and it is labelled where it appears. What the tiers mean is on how we check.

The clues

What the clues say.

Clues that it helps

  • Roozenbeek and colleagues (2022): five short videos on manipulation techniques. Six randomised experiments (n = 6,464) and a field study on YouTube (n = 22,632) found improved recognition of manipulation techniques, better discernment and better quality of sharing.

Clues that qualify it

  • Maertens and colleagues (2021) studied how long the benefit lasts. Their finding, as we have it, is that inoculation effects fade without reminders. We have not re-checked how fast.
  • Modirrousta-Galian and Higham (2023) reanalysed game-style inoculation studies and caution that the games may make people more doubtful of everything, rather than better at telling true from false.
  • Tier 3 A plausible lead. A preprint on prebunking videos in India (OSF) is reported to show a reach of 68.3 million YouTube users in late 2023, and in its final study only small, limited effects, with some videos backfiring. We saw its summary, not its tables, and it is not peer-reviewed.

The doubts

What we can't tell.

  • Most measures are recognition tasks or attitudes soon after the video, not what people forward weeks later.
  • A game and a video are different treatments. Evidence on one doesn't transfer automatically to the other.
  • The India evidence is thinner than the main study. We can't size “small”.
  • Languages, topics and audiences differ across these studies.

Where the clues point

“Protects” is stronger than the evidence supports. “Helps somewhat, for a while, in some settings” is closer. India's evidence is weaker still, and a Tier 3 lead at that. This is a leaning, not a verdict. It changes if the clues do.

Related: Would you act? and Can you teach someone not to be fooled?

Questions to ponder

Over to you.

  1. How would you decide a video had protected someone: what they say straight after, or what they forward a month later?
  2. If a lesson makes people doubt everything, has it helped?
  3. Why might a video work in one language and not another?
