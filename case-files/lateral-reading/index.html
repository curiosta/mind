Can you teach someone not to be fooled? Three studies

Three studies, three designs. They can't be lined up neatly, which is part of the case.

The claim

One lesson on spotting fake news makes people harder to fool.

Tier 1 · Primary Last checked 24 Oct 2026 (IST)

Why this tier: Three peer-reviewed studies, each opened by DOI. Abstracts read; we did not re-read the full papers or their effect sizes. What the tiers mean is on how we check.

The clues

What the clues say.

Clues against “one lesson is enough”

  • Badrinathan (2021): a field experiment in Bihar around the 2019 election (N = 1,224). A one-hour in-person media-literacy session did not significantly improve people's ability to identify misinformation on average, and ruling-party supporters became less able to spot false stories that matched their views.

Clues that teaching can work

  • Wineburg and colleagues (2022): six one-hour lessons in real classrooms. Students taught lateral reading (n = 271) improved significantly compared with a control group (n = 228). We quote no effect size.
  • Amar and colleagues (2025): a four-month classroom curriculum across 583 villages and 13,500 students in Bihar improved students' ability to tell true from false health information.

The doubts

What we can't tell.

  • Different places, ages, lengths and outcomes. We can't compare the three directly.
  • A longer programme differs in more than minutes. We can't tell from these abstracts whether length, content or follow-up made the difference.
  • The result about ruling-party supporters is a subgroup finding in one study at one election.
  • We have not read the effect sizes.

Where the clues point

A single short session is the weakest case in what we read. Longer, repeated teaching shows gains. We hold that loosely: three studies, three designs. This is a leaning, not a verdict. It changes if the clues do.

Questions to ponder

Over to you.

  1. What is the difference between knowing a rule and using it when a forward arrives late at night?
  2. A one-hour session left some people worse at spotting stories they already agreed with. What might be going on? (We don't know.)
  3. When did you last leave a page to find out who was behind it?
