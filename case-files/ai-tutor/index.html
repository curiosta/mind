Case file 03 · all case files

AI tutors: do chatbots help students learn, or just finish?

The interesting part of this case is that the same study reports both a gain and a harm.

The claim

Letting students practise with an AI chatbot helps them learn.

Tier 1 · Primary Last checked 24 Oct 2026 (IST)

Why this tier: The main clue is one peer-reviewed field experiment (PNAS, 2025), opened by DOI, abstract read. One study is a clue, not a verdict. A second clue is a preprint, and is labelled as such. What the tiers mean is on how we check.

The clues

What the clues say.

Clues the chatbot helps

  • Bastani and colleagues (2025) ran an experiment with about 1,000 high-school maths students. During practice, students with a plain GPT-4 chatbot did far better on practice problems (+48%), and those with a tutor version built with guardrails did better still (+127%).

Clues it can harm

  • When the AI was taken away, the group that had practised with the plain chatbot scored 17% worse than a group that never had AI. The tutor version's guardrails largely removed that harm.
  • Tier 3 A plausible lead, not a finding. A 2025 preprint (Kosmyna and colleagues, 54 essay writers, EEG) reported weaker connectivity, less sense of ownership and poorer recall of their own essay among chatbot users. It is a preprint and not peer-reviewed. Its authors' institution (MIT) describes it as preliminary, and a January 2026 comment raises concerns about sample size and EEG method. We list it because it is widely quoted, and we rely on nothing in it.

The doubts

What we can't tell.

  • One study, one subject (maths), one age group, and one AI model from the time of the study. Newer models and other subjects aren't covered.
  • We don't know whether this carries over to Indian classrooms.
  • “17% worse” is relative to a control group on one test, not a measure of how much anyone forgot.
  • We read the abstract, not the full paper.

Where the clues point

It depends on how the tool is set up. The same study found a gain and a harm, which points away from “AI always helps” and away from “AI always harms”. This is a leaning, not a verdict. It changes if the clues do.

Questions to ponder

Over to you.

  1. When you use a chatbot for homework, are you practising the thing or getting the thing?
  2. How would you tell, a week later, whether you learned the method or just saw it done?
  3. What would make you trust a result from one school's maths class for a subject like yours? And what would you want to see before repeating a preprint?
