The spellbook

Five spells. Each one is a drill.

A spell you cannot cast on a Tuesday morning is a costume. These are the drills. The names are so you remember them when you are tired.

00 · Where the names came from

I grew up on Harry Potter.

Not as a fan who collected trivia. As a kid who believed the world had a hidden layer — that a word, said right, could change what was real. I read the books the way you read a map of somewhere you intend to go.

Years later I noticed something uncomfortable. The spells that actually worked in my life were not the ones from the books. They were the ones the books had pointed at. A strong memory that no fake could overwrite. A pause before you act on a feeling. The ability to read what someone is leaving out. The habit of tracing a claim back to where it started. And the discipline of building an idea from the ground up instead of accepting the finished shape.

So I kept the names. Not as decoration — as handles. When you are tired, a name sticks better than a definition. The Patronus is easier to remember than "specific memory consolidation." The spell is the label. The skill underneath is the real thing.

This page is the map between the two.

1 · The Patronus

In the books, it is the only spell that works against a dementor — and it only works if you conjure a memory that is genuinely yours. Not a mood. A specific one. A place, a smell, a sentence someone said.

In real life, a dementor is a story that replaces your own. A generated photo of a place you never stood. A recap of a meeting you attended, written so smoothly you stop trusting your notes. The counter is a memory specific enough that a stranger could draw it.

Why it works

Oakley: memory consolidates in sleep, and rereading feels like knowing. A vague memory ("the trip was good") is easy to overwrite. A particular one is not.

Practice

  1. Once a day, write four lines: place, one smell or sound, one sentence someone said, what your hands were doing.
  2. Sleep on it. Next morning, rewrite from memory. Keep the version that still has the detail.
  3. Test: could someone else picture it? If not, it is a caption, not a memory.

Failure

Collecting photos and calling that remembering. The camera had the memory. You did not.

2 · Occlumency

In the books, it is the art of closing your mind to intrusion — of noticing when someone is reaching in and deciding what they get to see.

In real life, it is the pause between the feeling and the send. A claim that arrives already finished — no working, no source, just the sting — is using the feeling as proof. The share button is faster than the thought.

Why it works

Oakley and Sejnowski: anticipating an unpleasant task lights the same pain regions as the task itself. The pain drops once you start. The same body, used the other way, treats a forwarded outrage as urgent because the body already flinched.

Practice

  1. Before you reply or forward, name the feeling in one word. Anger, fear, flattery, rush.
  2. If you cannot name it, you are not ready to act on it.
  3. Wait until the word is written down. Then decide.

Failure

Calling the pause "overthinking" and sending anyway. The pause is the spell.

3 · Legilimency

In the books, it is reading minds — seeing what a person actually thinks beneath what they say.

In real life, it is reading the frame, not the sentence. What is repeated. What is skipped. Who is flattered. This includes you. A person who only reads one kind of piece will call the gap "common sense."

Why it works

The Einstellung effect: focused mode keeps returning to the same solution. Hutchinson's line, from the chemistry studies, is that most of us are inductive learners. We accept a claim once we can see where it came from. Hide the path, and the claim feels like a fact.

Practice

  1. On one piece you agree with, write the sentence they did not say.
  2. On one piece you reject, write the observation that would make you change your mind.
  3. If you cannot do either, you are inside the frame.

Failure

Using this only on other people. That is gossip, not a spell.

4 · Priori Incantatem

In the books, it is the moment two spells collide and the earlier one echoes through the later — the trace of where a magic came from.

In real life, it means: go back. A quote has a speaker and a date. A statistic has a table. A photo has a place it was taken, or it does not. The last spell leaves an echo of the earlier ones — and so does every claim.

Practice

  1. Find the earliest source you can, not the article that cites the article.
  2. For an image, search the image itself, not the caption.
  3. Compress what you found into one sentence before you pass it on. If the sentence needs "someone said," you do not have it yet.

Failure

Stopping at a familiar masthead. The masthead can be copied. The table cannot, if you open it.

5 · The Foundation

This one has no wand name, and that is the point. Hutchinson did not borrow a spell — he built a method. Foundation. Questions. Observations. Deductions. Model. In that order. A model that arrives first has already won, and you did not notice.

Practice

  1. Write the observation in a sentence that does not contain the conclusion.
  2. Write the question the observation forces.
  3. Only then write the model, and write what would break it.

Failure

You can state the model and nothing else. You accepted it. You did not build it. The learning page has the full order →
